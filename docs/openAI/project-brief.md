# Project Brief: License to Surf
## A playful, game-inspired way to learn digital skills

### 1. The idea

**License to Surf** is a free, non-commercial web app that helps people become more confident, capable, and safe when using the internet and digital devices.

The goal is to teach practical digital skills through short, entertaining, interactive experiences rather than traditional courses, long videos, or walls of text.

Imagine an old-school point-and-click adventure game blended with a modern educational experience. Players explore a fictional digital city, meet characters, visit locations, solve problems, and gradually earn their *License to Surf*.

The experience should feel like exploring a world—not completing corporate security training.

The project is intended for friends, family, and the general public, with particular consideration for children, teenagers, and older adults who may benefit from accessible digital education.

### 2. The guiding philosophy

The central idea is:

**Don't just tell people how to stay safe online. Let them explore, make decisions, learn from mistakes, and practice navigating the internet.**

The app should help users develop:
- Confidence using websites, browsers, computers, and everyday online services.
- The ability to recognize suspicious content, misleading websites, scams, and misinformation.
- Healthy habits around privacy, social media, online relationships, and digital communication.
- Critical thinking about what they see, share, believe, and interact with online.
- The ability to recover from mistakes and know what to do next.

The tone should be friendly, witty, empowering, and occasionally quirky. Avoid fearmongering, shame, condescension, and treating people who fall for scams as foolish.

This is not intended to be corporate security awareness software. Existing enterprise training products already serve that market.

### 3. The visual and creative direction

**Preferred direction: a retro point-and-click adventure set in an explorable city.**

Think late-1980s and early-1990s computer adventure games, with pixel art, memorable locations, strange characters, environmental storytelling, and a little dry humor. The style can evoke the era of classic adventure games without copying any particular game or using adult humor.

The city acts as the main navigation interface. The player sees a character standing at a crossroads, with different places to explore.

Possible locations:

- **Browser Café:** Browsers, search engines, URLs, tabs, bookmarks, downloads, and everyday web navigation.
- **Security Agency:** Phishing, suspicious links, passwords, passkeys, multi-factor authentication, permissions, and software updates.
- **Social Square:** Privacy, cyberbullying, group chats, online relationships, consent, peer pressure, and digital footprints.
- **Digital Library:** Search skills, trustworthy sources, misinformation, AI-generated content, deepfakes, and critical thinking.
- **Keyboard Arcade:** Typing, keyboard shortcuts, files, folders, and basic computer confidence.
- **License Office:** A final practical assessment and the player's License to Surf certificate.

These are starting concepts, not fixed requirements. The city should feel coherent, charming, and memorable rather than like a collection of unrelated course modules.

Include an accessible alternative to the map, such as a simple list of locations and missions. The game-inspired interface must not prevent users with disabilities or limited computer experience from completing the lessons.

### 4. The curriculum

Organize the content into short themed areas, each containing small missions that teach one or two practical skills.

#### World 1: Discover the Web
- Internet versus World Wide Web.
- Browsers, websites, search engines, and URLs.
- Tabs, bookmarks, downloads, files, and folders.
- Finding information efficiently.
- Everyday computer and keyboard skills.

#### World 2: Cyber Detective
- Recognizing phishing and suspicious messages.
- Understanding domains, hostnames, and misleading URLs.
- Creating and managing strong, unique passwords.
- Passkeys and multi-factor authentication.
- Browser warnings, permissions, updates, and privacy settings.
- What to do after clicking a suspicious link or sharing information accidentally.

A signature challenge could be called **Find the Impostor**. Players investigate several URLs and determine which website they are actually visiting. For example:

`https://microsoft.com.attacker.example/login`

The lesson explains that the hostname is `microsoft.com.attacker.example`, not Microsoft's official domain. It should also teach that HTTPS alone does not mean a website is trustworthy.

#### World 3: Navigate Social Media
- Privacy settings and location sharing.
- Cyberbullying, bystander behavior, and reporting.
- Consent, screenshots, and sharing other people's content.
- Group chats, peer pressure, and online relationships.
- Influencers, sponsored content, engagement algorithms, and manipulation.
- Fake profiles, impersonation, deepfakes, and AI-generated media.
- Digital footprints and the consequences of posting or sharing.
- Misinformation, emotional manipulation, and viral claims.

This world should be especially thoughtful about young people's real experiences. Scenarios should allow players to consider their options without implying that every difficult situation has one simple answer.

#### World 4: Live Your Digital Life
- Email, attachments, video calls, and online communication.
- Online shopping, payment scams, and suspicious offers.
- Account recovery and securing compromised accounts.
- Evaluating sources and checking claims.
- Using AI tools thoughtfully and verifying their outputs.
- Solving everyday digital problems independently.

#### World 5: Earn Your License
A final adventure combines skills from the previous worlds. Instead of asking players to repeat definitions, it presents realistic situations that require them to make decisions.

For example, a player might receive a suspicious message, investigate a website, evaluate a viral claim, and decide how to respond to a troubling group-chat interaction.

Successful completion unlocks a personalized **License to Surf** certificate.

The curriculum should be modular. The project does not need to launch with every world fully developed.

### 5. Gamification: make learning compelling

Gamification should create curiosity, a sense of discovery, and satisfying progress—not turn education into a grind.

Potential mechanics include:

**Exploration and unlocking**
- Players explore a city map and discover new locations.
- Completing missions opens new areas, characters, objects, and storylines.
- Some characters can return in later missions, giving the city continuity.

**Short missions**
- Each mission takes approximately 2–5 minutes.
- Missions begin with a recognizable situation, present a decision or puzzle, and end with feedback.
- Players learn by doing, rather than reading a lesson before reaching the interesting part.

**A digital passport**
- Players collect stamps, badges, or credentials for demonstrated skills.
- Examples: URL Detective, Privacy Navigator, Scam Spotter, Fact Checker, and Kind Communicator.
- The passport provides a visible record of progress toward the final license.

**Branching scenarios**
- Decisions affect what happens next.
- Characters react to the player's choices.
- Different choices can produce different consequences and explanations.
- There can be more than one reasonable response in nuanced social situations.

**Tools and collectibles**
- Players gradually build a small inventory of useful digital tools: a magnifying glass for investigating URLs, a checklist for verifying information, or a privacy toolkit.
- Items should reinforce useful mental models, not merely act as decorative rewards.

**Mysteries and environmental storytelling**
- Characters need help solving problems.
- Clues are hidden in websites, messages, profiles, and objects around the city.
- Recurring mysteries connect otherwise separate lessons.

**Feedback, hints, and retries**
- Explain why an answer is useful or risky.
- Offer hints without making players feel stupid.
- Let players retry immediately.
- Reward good reasoning and improvement, not just getting the right answer on the first attempt.

**A final license**
- The certificate represents skills demonstrated in practical challenges.
- It can be printable or shareable.
- It should clearly be an informal educational achievement, not an official professional qualification.

Avoid mechanics that undermine the project's welcoming nature: public leaderboards, streak anxiety, punitive failure screens, unnecessary time pressure, and notifications designed to create compulsive engagement. The objective is digital independence, not maximizing time spent in the app.

### 6. Instructional design

Each mission should follow a repeatable pattern:

1. **Encounter:** The player arrives in a situation or meets a character with a problem.
2. **Investigate:** The player examines a message, URL, profile, website, or other evidence.
3. **Decide:** The player chooses what to do.
4. **Experience:** The game shows the likely consequences.
5. **Understand:** Brief feedback explains the underlying principle.
6. **Try again or transfer:** The player retries if needed, then encounters a slightly different example to check whether they can apply the lesson.

The most important test is whether the player can use the skill in a new situation, not whether they remember the lesson's wording.

Keep explanations concise and use realistic examples, dialogue, visual clues, humor, and interactive demonstrations. Make it easy to stop and resume later.

### 7. Use existing research and educational resources

A key project principle is **do not reinvent the wheel**.

Use established frameworks and educational material to identify learning objectives, validate explanations, and avoid overlooking important topics. Then create original game scenarios, characters, puzzles, and dialogue to make the content engaging.

Useful starting points:

- **European Commission — DigComp:** https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/digcomp_en  
  A framework for digital competence, including information literacy, communication, safety, and problem-solving.

- **ENISA CyberEducation:** https://www.enisa.europa.eu/topics/education-and-career-path/cybereducation-platform  
  European cybersecurity education resources and initiatives.

- **ENISA Awareness and Cyber Hygiene:** https://www.enisa.europa.eu/topics/awareness-and-cyber-hygiene  
  Resources for cybersecurity awareness and practical habits.

- **Better Internet for Kids:** https://better-internet-for-kids.europa.eu/en  
  Resources concerning children's online safety, privacy, cyberbullying, and online relationships.

- **Google Be Internet Awesome / Interland:** https://beinternetawesome.withgoogle.com/  
  An example of playful digital-safety education and interactive learning.

- **CyberNinja:** https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/learning-space/resources/cyberninja  
  Another potential source of inspiration for game-based cybersecurity education.

These resources are starting points, not a claim that any one of them supplies the entire curriculum. Investigate their specific learning objectives, audiences, strengths, limitations, and content licenses.

Before reusing text, illustrations, activities, or other material, check the relevant licensing terms. Prefer original scenarios informed by reputable research over copying another project's content.

A useful research output would be a curriculum matrix containing:

| Topic | Learning objective | Existing reference | Original mission | Evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Misleading URLs | Identify the actual hostname and destination | Relevant digital-literacy and cybersecurity guidance | Find the Impostor | Correctly analyzes unfamiliar URLs |
| Cyberbullying | Identify constructive responses and support options | Better Internet for Kids | A group-chat dilemma | Chooses a reasonable response and identifies support |
| Misinformation | Evaluate a claim and verify its source | DigComp and relevant media-literacy guidance | The Viral Rumor | Checks evidence rather than trusting popularity |
| Password security | Select safer account-protection practices | ENISA guidance | The Account Locksmith | Chooses an appropriate protection strategy |

The table is illustrative. Each entry should be researched and linked to a specific, suitable source before it is treated as final curriculum.

### 8. Accessibility, inclusion, and privacy

The experience should work for children, teenagers, adults, and older adults without assuming a particular level of technical knowledge.

Priorities:
- Plain language, with technical terms explained in context.
- Readable text, good contrast, scalable fonts, and clear controls.
- Keyboard navigation and screen-reader compatibility.
- Instructions that do not depend on color alone.
- Optional hints and adjustable difficulty.
- No requirement to finish within a time limit.
- No real credentials, sensitive personal information, or risky actions required to play.
- Safe, fictional examples rather than live phishing links or real targets.
- No public display of players' performance by default.
- Minimal data collection and a privacy-conscious approach, particularly for minors.

For difficult social topics, provide supportive language and practical next steps. The app should help players identify when they may need a trusted adult, platform reporting tools, or other appropriate support, without pretending that a game can resolve every real-world problem.

### 9. Suggested MVP

Do not build the entire game immediately. First validate whether the central idea is genuinely fun and educational.

**Phase 1 — Research**
- Review DigComp, ENISA, and Better Internet for Kids.
- Identify the most valuable skills for the initial audience.
- Create a curriculum matrix with learning objectives and source references.
- Establish an original visual identity and content reuse policy.

**Phase 2 — Playable prototype**
- Build one attractive city-map screen with five explorable locations.
- Implement one complete mission: Find the Impostor.
- Include investigation, decisions, hints, feedback, retries, and a passport stamp.
- Provide a simple alternative to map navigation.

**Phase 3 — User testing**
- Test with a small, varied group of willing users, including people with different levels of digital confidence.
- Observe whether they understand the interface without extensive instructions.
- Ask whether they want to explore another location.
- Test whether they can apply what they learned to a new example.
- Check accessibility and whether any feedback feels patronizing or confusing.

**Phase 4 — Expand selectively**
- Add one social-media-literacy mission and one phishing or scam mission.
- Improve the mechanics based on testing.
- Develop the certificate and additional city locations once the core loop works.

The MVP succeeds if people voluntarily play, understand the scenarios, learn something useful, and can apply it beyond the game—not simply if they complete the content.

### 10. Technical direction

The creator is a software developer, so implementation can be chosen based on personal strengths rather than an imposed technology stack.

Useful architectural principles:
- Store lessons and scenarios as structured, reusable content rather than hardcoding every lesson into a separate page.
- Separate the learning content from the game interface.
- Make branching scenarios and feedback easy to author and revise.
- Support saving progress without requiring an account in the initial version if feasible.
- Keep the first release small, fast, responsive, and inexpensive to host.
- Avoid building a backend, social network, or complex account system unless testing demonstrates a genuine need.

A small library of reusable dialogue, character, map, and challenge components will be more valuable initially than a large custom game engine.

### 11. Key questions for further exploration

The concept is promising, but several decisions deserve scrutiny:

- Who should be the primary audience for the first release: teenagers, adults with limited digital confidence, or a mixed audience?
- Should the city be a continuous narrative adventure or a set of largely independent missions?
- Which mechanics genuinely improve learning, and which are just decoration?
- How can one visual style appeal to children without feeling childish to adults?
- What is the right balance between general digital literacy, cybersecurity, and social-media literacy?
- How can the app measure learning without invasive tracking or unnecessary personal data?
- What accessibility standards and evaluation methods should guide development?
- Which existing educational resources are most authoritative and reusable?
- What is the smallest prototype that can validate both engagement and learning?
- How can the project remain free, sustainable, and easy to maintain?
